Each metric answers a different question, and almost every wrong conclusion comes from reading one in isolation. A module can show a full spectrum because it is capacity-bound, because its input is narrow, because a nonlinearity inflated the rank, or because it was never trained. Sorting between those four is what the verdict engine does, and what this guide is for.

Everything here is measured on the frozen checkpoint you handed in: forward passes over the batches you supplied, plus SVD on the existing weights. Nothing is trained.

Rank and saturation metrics

All of these come from the singular values of the module's activations, collected as an [N, D] matrix over the captured batches (rows subsampled to about 4096 in total) and mean-centred, so they are a PCA spectrum. They differ in how much weight they give the tail, which is why they disagree and why the disagreement is informative.

MetricWhat it measuresRangeWhere it misleads
Effective rankexp(H), H the Shannon entropy of the normalised singular values1 to DInflated by any nonlinearity: a ReLU on a rank-8 input can read far above 8. Never a verdict input on its own.
Stable rankΣσ² / σmax²1 to DIgnores the tail almost entirely, so it reads low whenever one direction dominates even when many others carry real signal.
Participation ratio(Σλ)² / Σλ², λ = σ²1 to DAgrees with effective rank on clean spectra, diverges on heavy tails.
Numerical rankCount of σ above σ_max · max(N, D) · ε₃₂0 to DA float-precision count. It saturates at D long before the module uses D dimensions well.
Rank at 90 % / 99 % energyDirections needed to reconstruct 90 % / 99 % of the variance1 to DThe most robust activation-only measures, but blind to whether the loss needs those directions.

When they disagree, trust rank at 99 % energy over effective rank, and trust the truncation sweep over both. A large gap between numerical rank and rank at 90 % energy means a long low-energy tail: many directions carrying a little signal each, which is what a module looks like just before it runs out of room.

The activation singular-value chart is worth reading directly. A sharp cliff means the module lives in far fewer dimensions than it has. A smooth decay reaching the noise floor only near D means there is no spare subspace to reclaim.

The truncation sweep

This is the strongest signal the tool produces, because it is the only one that asks the loss rather than the activations. It runs only when you pass loss_fn. For each module, every matrix weight inside it (all of them jointly, for a container) is replaced by its rank-k approximation, k is swept on a log grid and then bisected, the loss is measured on the first few batches, and the weights are restored.

  • used rank is the smallest k that keeps the loss within rel_tol (1 %) of the base loss. If there is no sweep, the detail panel falls back to rank at 99 % energy and says so in the used rank cell's tooltip.
  • used % is used rank divided by the module's output width. This is what the verdict thresholds are applied to.
  • marginal pressure is the relative loss increase when the module loses its single last used dimension (the bisection always evaluates used − 1). A module whose last dimension costs 30 % of the loss is under pressure; one that costs 1 % is not, even at the same used %.

Weight rank cap is the field that stops this being misread, and it matters more than the percentage. A Linear(15 → 128) has weight rank capped at 15 by its input, so "the loss needs all 15 directions" is a statement about the 15-dimensional input, not about whether 128 outputs are enough. The tool tags each module by the weight producing its output:

  • output-capped or square with a high used %: genuine evidence to widen this module.
  • input-capped with a high used %: evidence to widen whatever feeds it. The verdict engine routes this to upstream and discounts the priority.
  • For a container, the cap comes from the leaf that produces its output, not the narrowest weight anywhere inside it. A four-layer stack whose first layer takes 15 inputs is not "capped at 15" as a whole; the nonlinearities in between let later layers use their full width, and the sweep measures that.

Two cautions. The sweep is the expensive part of a run (one forward pass over the sweep batches per rank step per module), so on a large model start without loss_fn, read the activation ranks, then sweep. And on an undertrained checkpoint it reports used rank 1 almost everywhere, because destroying weights costs nothing when the model has not learned anything. The report detects that pattern (half or more of the swept leaves at rank ≤ 1) and puts a warning in the header instead of reporting confident verdicts.

Weight spectrum alpha

Alpha comes from the weights alone and needs no data, so it is the one thing the data-free mode reports. Take the eigenvalues λ of WᵀW in descending order and fit a power law to the tail with the Hill estimator over the top tenth (k ≈ n/10, at least 1):

alpha = 1 + k / Σi≤k log(λi / λk+1)

The interpretation follows Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularisation (Martin and Mahoney), where alpha tracks how far a layer's spectrum has moved from its random initialisation:

AlphaWhat it meansWhat to do
above 6Spectrum still close to the random baseline; little task structure has formedMore steps, more data or a different learning rate. Not more width.
2 to 6Correlations have built up; the layer is well conditionedJudge capacity on the rank metrics and the sweep; this layer is ready to be assessed.
below 2Over-trained, tending toward memorisationRegularisation or more data, not more width.

Alpha earns its place because it separates two states that look identical in the rank metrics: a layer that has not learned anything and a layer that has learned everything it can both show a saturated-looking spectrum. Alpha tells you which one you are looking at.

It is noisy on small matrices. Convolutions are viewed as [out, in·k·k]. A module's alpha is the median over its matrix weights; the gauge shows each weight as a dot. Treat values from matrices with fewer than about 50 eigenvalues as indicative rather than precise, and read the head layers' alpha with that in mind. If the median alpha over all leaves is above 6, the header warns that the whole checkpoint looks undertrained.

Graph metrics: Dirichlet energy and MAD

These run only when you pass edge_index_fn, and only for modules whose output is node-aligned (one row per node of the batch). They answer a different question from everything else here: not "is this module out of room" but "has message passing destroyed the distinctions between nodes". Widening cannot fix oversmoothing, so a module diagnosed this way is taken off the growth list entirely.

Dirichlet energy is computed on centred features: the mean over edges of ‖xi − xj‖² divided by the mean over nodes of ‖xi − x̄‖². Random independent features give about 2, perfectly smoothed features give 0. MAD is the mean cosine distance to neighbours on the raw features.

Read the decay, never the level. A common offset makes any normalised energy look small without smoothing having occurred: post-ReLU activations all sit in the positive orthant. Centring removes the offset, and the verdict still only uses the ratio to the nearest upstream module that has a Dirichlet reading (shown as dirichlet ref in the metric grid). Oversmoothed fires only when energy is below 0.05 in absolute terms and below 30 % of that upstream value.

MAD is shown but not trusted. When every feature dimension shares a sign, cosine distances collapse toward zero regardless of how distinct the nodes are, which after a ReLU is the normal state. Use it as corroboration for a Dirichlet decay, never on its own.

Redundancy and unit health

These find width that is already present and going unused, which is the counter-evidence to every saturation reading: a module cannot be short of capacity while wasting a third of what it has.

Linear CKA measures whether two modules hold the same representation, computed over identically sampled rows:

CKA(X, Y) = ‖XᵀY‖²F / (‖XᵀX‖F ‖YᵀY‖F)

Above 0.98 two modules are doing one job, and adding width to either is unlikely to pay. In the similarity heatmap, look for dark square blocks along the diagonal: a run of consecutive modules collapsed onto one stage of computation.

The best unrelated CKA cell and the redundant verdict exclude two kinds of partner: relatives (a Sequential returns exactly its last child's output, so parent and child trivially score 1.0) and anything downstream of the module (a consumer that merely copies its input is the consumer's problem, not the producer's). The heatmap and the neighbour list still show those pairs.

Dead units are output dimensions whose variance across samples is essentially zero (below 10⁻⁶ of the mean unit variance). Duplicate units are dimensions whose absolute correlation with an earlier dimension exceeds 0.95. The duplicate signal is the stronger one: a dead unit may be an unlucky initialisation, but a duplicate is width the optimiser actively chose to spend on a copy. Either fraction summed above 35 % gives a redundant verdict that pre-empts saturation. Below about 10 %, neither is telling you much.

The activation histogram is the sanity check for when a module's numbers seem wrong. A spike at zero taller than everything else (see zeros in its title) means a mostly-dead ReLU. Values pinned at the extremes of a bounded activation mean saturation in the classical sense. A large offset from zero explains a suspiciously low raw MAD.

The dataflow graph and upstream carry

The graph is the runtime dataflow, recovered by walking the autograd graph backwards from each module's inputs to the nearest module that produced them. That is why functional ops (F.relu, index_add_, scatter, concatenation) do not break it: they are simply steps on the walk. Nodes are the leaves plus every container that builds its own output rather than handing a child's through, such as a SAGEConv returning lin_l(agg) + lin_r(x); those are labelled "container that computes its own output" and drawn as a box inside their own hull. Container-level edges are projections of node edges, and the root is never a node.

Upstream carry is the narrowest point along the paths feeding a module: carry(M) = min(in_dim(M), Σproducers P min(width(P), carry(P))), walked in execution order. A source module carries its own input width. A nonlinearity cannot add information, so a ReLU's contribution is exactly its carry regardless of its measured rank. This encodes a constraint none of the per-module metrics can see: a module downstream of an 8-dimensional path cannot hold more than 8 dimensions of information however wide it is, even though a ReLU on that path can show an effective rank of 40.

Two limits of carry to keep in mind. Producers are summed, which is right for a concatenation or a sum of maps of different tensors (a SAGE layer reading x and the neighbour mean of x genuinely has up to twice the rank), and an over-estimate when several producers carry the same information. And it treats message passing as pointwise: aggregation brings in other nodes' features, which carry does not count. So carry is a floor on what could be missing upstream, not an exact figure.

Verdicts and priority

Evaluated in order; the first match wins. Every verdict above saturated describes a condition under which a module looks capacity-bound but is not, which is why they pre-empt it. The thresholds are in capscope.THRESH and can be overridden per run with inspect(thresholds=...); the values used are recorded in the report. If you change them, keep the order.

upstreamCarry is below the width and used rank is within 10 % (± 1) of the carry; for a leaf the used rank is first capped by its weight rankIt uses everything that arrives, so it is relaying, not saturating. The sentence says where to widen instead (the named upstream module, or the raw input).

Priority is the 0 to 1 score the module list sorts on. It is a ranking device, not a measurement: read the ordering, not the number. Saturated scores 0.70 plus up to 0.15 for used % and 0.15 for marginal pressure; tight 0.35 plus up to 0.20 for how far past 60 % it is and 0.10 for pressure; spare up to 0.15; upstream 0.12, undertrained 0.08, redundant and oversmoothed 0.05; passthrough and narrow 0. Input-capped saturated or tight modules are multiplied by 0.6 (widen upstream instead), and any output layer (a sink of the graph) by 0.5, because its width is fixed by the task.

Reading them together

No single metric decides anything. A full-looking spectrum is the entry point, not the conclusion, and four checks stand between it and a decision to spend parameters: is the checkpoint trained (alpha, the header warning), is the module relaying a narrower input (carry, rank cap), is its width already wasted (dead and duplicate units, CKA), and is the loss actually pressing on its last dimensions (the sweep, marginal pressure). Only the path through all four ends in more width.

Run it in this order across the whole model rather than module by module:

  1. Check the training loss first, outside this tool. If training loss is also stuck, the problem is capacity or optimisation. If training loss is low while validation is flat, no amount of width will help and you are looking at the wrong problem.
  2. Read the header. An undertrained warning invalidates every other reading. A "no dataflow edges" warning means the graph and the carry are missing, so the upstream verdict could not fire.
  3. Scan the alpha column for anything above 6.
  4. Take the top three or four by priority and confirm each with its truncation sweep chart: the used-rank marker should sit near the right edge with the loss climbing steeply to its left. A flat curve means the loss does not care, whatever the activation rank says.
  5. Check the rank cap and the carry of each candidate before committing. Input-capped means the widening belongs one layer earlier.

What none of these can tell you

Every metric here is descriptive. They characterise the state a trained model has arrived at; none of them predicts what a larger model would do.

  • How much to add. A saturated module tells you the direction, not the size. Size estimates need gradient-based growth criteria (the gap between the update backpropagation asks for and the one the layer can realise), which need a backward pass and are not in the tool.
  • Whether the extra width will be used. Saturation says the current width is fully occupied, not that the task has more structure waiting to be captured.
  • Task interference between heads. Nothing here sees it. For a multi-head model, take the pairwise cosine similarity of each head's gradients with respect to the shared trunk; persistently negative similarity means the plateau is conflict rather than capacity.
  • Depth versus width. Every metric here is about a module's dimensionality. Whether the model needs more layers, more hops, or a different aggregation is outside what any of it measures.
  • Data limits. If the plateau comes from label noise or from the task being saturated at your dataset size, the whole model will look healthy and no verdict will fire.
